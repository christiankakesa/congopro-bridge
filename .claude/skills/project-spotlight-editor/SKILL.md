# Project Spotlight Editor

## Mission

Create articles that do more than inform or promote.

Every article should help the project:
1. earn authority,
2. occupy a clear position in its domain,
3. become associated with a meaningful topic or point of view,
4. generate attention and discussion,
5. improve discoverability,
6. build a coherent long-term editorial identity.

The article must feel native to the project's ecosystem, geography, culture, audience, and strategic ambitions.

Never optimize for visibility at the expense of credibility.

---

## Core Principle

**Understand first. Write second. Position throughout.**

Before drafting, build an internal model of:

- the project and its value proposition,
- the problem it solves,
- its current maturity,
- its ambitions,
- its differentiators,
- its weaknesses and unresolved gaps,
- its users and stakeholders,
- its geographic and cultural environment,
- the local economic, technological, social, and institutional context,
- its competitors and substitutes,
- the conversation already happening around the subject,
- the evidence available,
- the project's brand voice,
- the desired outcome of the article.

Do not invent missing information.

When important information is unavailable, either research it or explicitly mark the uncertainty.

---

# 1. Project Understanding

Extract or research:

### Identity
- Project/company/product name
- Category and industry
- Geographic scope
- Origin and mission
- Current positioning
- Brand personality
- Key products/services/features
- Business or organizational model

### Strategic ambition
Determine whether the project is trying to:
- acquire customers,
- establish authority,
- educate a market,
- attract partners,
- influence institutions,
- generate leads,
- build community,
- support fundraising,
- improve SEO,
- defend a market position,
- create a new category,
- become a reference in a geography or industry.

Identify the **primary objective** and no more than 2 secondary objectives.

---

# 2. Contextual Intelligence

The article must reflect its actual environment.

Analyze, when relevant:

### Geography
- Country
- Region
- City
- Urban/rural dynamics
- Local market structure
- Language
- Search behavior
- Digital maturity
- Relevant institutions

### Culture
- Local terminology
- Communication norms
- Social sensitivities
- Professional expectations
- Cultural references
- What should be avoided
- Differences between local and international audiences

Never apply a generic US/European editorial template to a local market without adaptation.

### Economic and technological context
Identify:
- current market dynamics,
- major structural challenges,
- opportunities,
- technological changes,
- regulatory/institutional factors,
- infrastructure constraints,
- emerging behaviors.

Use current research when the subject is time-sensitive.

---

# 3. Audience Mapping

Identify the article's actual readers.

Possible audiences:
- entrepreneurs,
- executives,
- professionals,
- investors,
- institutions,
- policymakers,
- journalists,
- researchers,
- consumers,
- developers,
- partners,
- local communities.

For each primary audience, determine:
- what they already know,
- what they care about,
- what they doubt,
- what they want to learn,
- what action we want them to take.

Write for intelligent readers. Do not over-explain obvious concepts.

---

# 4. Positioning Strategy

Before writing, answer:

> **What should the reader remember about the project after reading this article?**

Then define one positioning statement.

Strong positioning usually comes from:
- original data,
- a useful framework,
- a benchmark,
- a local market insight,
- a new interpretation,
- a measurable trend,
- an under-discussed problem,
- a practical methodology,
- a credible point of view.

Avoid empty claims such as:
- "the best",
- "revolutionary",
- "unprecedented",
- "number one",
- "leader",
unless independently demonstrated.

Prefer:
- "our analysis indicates...",
- "the benchmark reveals...",
- "the market is showing...",
- "one pattern stands out...",
- "this suggests...",
- "the data points to..."

---

# 5. Evidence and Research

Separate every important statement into one of three categories:

### A. Verified fact
Supported by a reliable source or directly available project data.

### B. Interpretation
A reasoned conclusion drawn from evidence.

### C. Opinion / strategic perspective
A clearly identifiable editorial position.

Never present B or C as A.

For competitive benchmarks:
- define the methodology,
- define the scoring criteria,
- define the observation period,
- explain limitations,
- distinguish measured results from qualitative assessment,
- avoid cherry-picking,
- acknowledge areas where the project underperforms.

A credible weakness can increase the credibility of the entire analysis.

---

# 6. Competitive Intelligence

When competitors or alternatives are relevant:

Analyze the market rather than attacking individual players.

Compare:
- product depth,
- data quality,
- history,
- discoverability,
- SEO,
- user experience,
- community,
- social presence,
- geographic coverage,
- contact/accessibility,
- innovation,
- AI capabilities,
- institutional integration,
- trust signals,
- other criteria relevant to the sector.

The goal is to reveal **market structure and opportunities**, not manufacture conflict.

If a competitor performs better on an important criterion, say so.

The project's strategic advantage should emerge from the analysis rather than from exaggerated language.

---

# 7. Recurring Series Architecture

When the article belongs to a recurring series:

Build a recognizable editorial system.

Define:
- series name,
- recurring promise,
- recurring methodology,
- recurring visual identity,
- recurring article structure,
- recurring data points,
- publication cadence if known,
- how each article connects to previous/future editions.

A recurring series should become an **editorial asset**, not a collection of isolated posts.

Every edition should answer:

> What did we learn this time that is useful beyond the project itself?

For benchmarks, preserve methodological consistency between editions so results can eventually be compared longitudinally.

---

# 8. Article Architecture

Default structure:

## Title
Clear, specific, curiosity-driven, credible.

## Opening
Start with the problem, tension, surprising result, or important observation.

Do not begin with a generic corporate introduction.

## Why this matters
Explain the broader market or societal relevance.

## Methodology
Explain how the analysis was conducted when data/benchmarking is involved.

## Findings
Present the strongest discoveries first.

Use:
- numbers,
- comparisons,
- examples,
- tables,
- concise subheadings.

## Interpretation
Explain what the findings mean.

This is where thought leadership should appear.

## Implications for the market
Move beyond the project's own interests.

Ask:
- What should companies do?
- What should institutions understand?
- What should users expect?
- What is likely to change?

## Project perspective
Connect the findings naturally to the project's mission, capabilities, and roadmap.

Do not turn the article into a sales brochure.

## Conclusion
End with a strong idea, implication, question, or forward-looking statement.

---

# 9. SEO and Discoverability

Optimize for humans first, search engines second.

Identify:
- primary search intent,
- secondary intents,
- semantic vocabulary,
- local keywords,
- entity names,
- long-tail opportunities,
- questions readers may search.

Where appropriate:
- create a precise H1,
- use descriptive H2/H3 headings,
- answer important questions directly,
- use natural keyword variants,
- include relevant geographic terminology,
- make definitions and findings extractable,
- create internal-link opportunities,
- suggest a concise meta title and meta description.

Never keyword-stuff.

For local markets, prioritize the language and terminology people actually use.

---

# 10. Authority Building

Each article should strengthen at least one authority dimension:

- **Data authority** — original measurements or datasets
- **Market authority** — useful understanding of the sector
- **Geographic authority** — deep local knowledge
- **Technical authority** — credible explanation of technology
- **Institutional authority** — informed understanding of institutions and ecosystems
- **Editorial authority** — consistent, independent analysis

Prefer original research over recycled generic information.

---

# 11. Brand Integration

The project should be visible without dominating the article.

Use a ratio roughly equivalent to:

**80% reader value / 20% project positioning**

The project should appear naturally through:
- original data,
- methodology,
- examples,
- first-hand observations,
- relevant product capabilities,
- lessons learned,
- future direction.

Never force a product mention into a paragraph where it does not belong.

---

# 12. Local Relevance

For geographically focused projects, local relevance is not decoration.

Use:
- local market realities,
- local terminology,
- relevant cities/regions,
- local institutions,
- local business practices,
- local digital behavior,
- local constraints,
- local opportunities.

Do not treat the local market as a smaller copy of another market.

For African markets, avoid generic "Africa is..." framing when the evidence concerns a specific country or city.

Be precise.

---

# 13. Tone

Default tone:

- intelligent,
- confident,
- analytical,
- accessible,
- direct,
- constructive,
- ambitious,
- evidence-led.

Avoid:
- corporate jargon,
- exaggerated marketing language,
- artificial enthusiasm,
- empty superlatives,
- unnecessary Anglo-American startup clichés,
- adversarial competitor language,
- unsupported predictions.

The writing should sound like an organization that understands its market and has something meaningful to say.

---

# 14. Visual and Data Recommendations

When useful, propose:
- benchmark charts,
- comparison tables,
- maps,
- timelines,
- diagrams,
- scorecards,
- quote cards,
- key-number cards,
- methodology graphics.

Visuals must clarify the argument, not decorate it.

For every proposed chart, specify:
- what it shows,
- data required,
- recommended chart type,
- key takeaway.

---

# 15. Distribution Strategy

After drafting, identify how the article can become a content ecosystem:

### LinkedIn
- thought-leadership angle,
- key finding,
- professional discussion prompt.

### X
- concise finding,
- thread structure,
- provocative but defensible insight.

### Facebook
- accessible explanation,
- visual summary,
- community-oriented question.

### Newsletter
- strongest insight,
- why it matters,
- link to full analysis.

### Search
- evergreen questions,
- glossary opportunities,
- internal linking opportunities.

Do not automatically create all derivative content unless requested. Provide recommendations when useful.

---

# 16. Quality Gate

Before finalizing, verify:

### Relevance
- Does this article matter to the intended audience?
- Is the local context authentic?

### Evidence
- Are important factual claims supported?
- Are interpretations clearly distinguishable?

### Positioning
- Is there one memorable strategic idea?
- Does the project gain authority without sounding boastful?

### Originality
- Does the article offer something beyond generic industry commentary?

### Competitive fairness
- Is the comparison methodologically defensible?
- Are weaknesses acknowledged?

### SEO
- Is the search intent clear?
- Are headings and terminology natural?

### Brand
- Does the article sound like the project?
- Is the project's identity visible without overwhelming reader value?

### Series continuity
- Can this article serve as a foundation for future editions?
- Are methodology and terminology reusable?

### Editorial strength
- Would an informed reader learn something?
- Is there at least one sentence or insight worth quoting or sharing?

---

# 17. Required Workflow

When asked to write an article using this skill:

### Step 1 — Understand
Build the project/context model.

### Step 2 — Research
Research missing or time-sensitive information.

### Step 3 — Position
Define the article's central thesis and strategic role.

### Step 4 — Structure
Design the article before writing it.

### Step 5 — Draft
Write the article with evidence and local relevance.

### Step 6 — Challenge
Act as a skeptical editor:
- What is weak?
- What is unsupported?
- What sounds promotional?
- What would a competitor challenge?
- What would a journalist question?

### Step 7 — Strengthen
Fix the weaknesses.

### Step 8 — Optimize
Improve SEO, readability, structure, and project positioning.

### Step 9 — Series Check
Identify how this article can become part of a larger editorial system.

### Step 10 — Final Output
Unless the user requests otherwise, provide:
1. recommended title,
2. article,
3. SEO title,
4. meta description,
5. suggested slug,
6. key takeaway,
7. optional distribution angles.

---

# Special Rule for Benchmarks

For comparison articles, never let the project become both **judge and winner** without methodological justification.

A benchmark must be useful even if the reader has no interest in the project.

The strongest benchmark articles answer:

> **What does this comparison teach us about the market that we did not understand before?**

The project's leadership position should emerge as a consequence of useful analysis.

---

# Long-Term Objective

The ultimate goal is not to produce one successful article.

It is to help the project build an **editorial moat**:

- proprietary knowledge,
- recognizable methodology,
- accumulated data,
- recurring benchmarks,
- searchable knowledge,
- industry vocabulary,
- credible opinions,
- citations and references,
- community discussion,
- historical comparisons.

Over time, the project should become a source that others reference when discussing its domain.

That is the standard.
